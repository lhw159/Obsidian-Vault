# Stance-Phase Knee Flexion in Medial Knee Osteoarthritis and High Tibial Osteotomy: A Comprehensive Evidence Review

## Executive Summary

This report synthesizes evidence on stance-phase knee flexion dynamics in medial knee osteoarthritis (OA) and following high tibial osteotomy (HTO), with emphasis on the loading-response flexion excursion—the sagittal-plane knee motion from heel strike to the first flexion peak in early stance. Patients with medial knee OA consistently demonstrate reduced loading-response flexion excursion compared to healthy controls, typically exhibiting values of 1–7° versus the normal 10–15° range. This reduction is driven by quadriceps avoidance, pain avoidance, quadriceps weakness, and altered loading strategies that shift mechanical stress to the frontal plane. High tibial osteotomy reliably corrects coronal-plane alignment and reduces the knee adduction moment by approximately 40%, with mean mechanical axis corrections of 8–10°. However, evidence regarding sagittal-plane recovery is conflicting: Lind et al. (2013) reported normalization of stance-phase knee flexion at 12 months post-HTO, while Whatling et al. (2020) found persistent sagittal deficits despite frontal-plane improvements. Mechanisms underlying persistent post-HTO reduction include incomplete quadriceps recovery, habituated neuromuscular patterns, altered posterior tibial slope, and ongoing arthrogenic inhibition. Assessment of user-provided data (pre-operative loading-response excursion 1–7°, post-operative 0.1–7°) confirms these values are consistent with the literature, showing the expected pre-operative reduction and the documented heterogeneity in post-operative recovery. Individual patient outcomes ranged from marked improvement (+3.2°) to substantial worsening (−3.9°), reflecting the variable nature of sagittal-plane adaptation after HTO.

## 1. Introduction

Medial compartment knee osteoarthritis is characterized by progressive cartilage degeneration, pain, and altered gait mechanics. Patients with medial knee OA commonly adopt **compensatory movement strategies** that **reduce dynamic loading on the diseased compartment, including reduced knee flexion during the loading-response phase of gait.** High tibial osteotomy is a joint-preserving surgical intervention that corrects varus malalignment and redistributes load from the medial to the lateral compartment. While HTO reliably improves frontal-plane mechanics and patient-reported outcomes, its effect on sagittal-plane kinematics—particularly the loading-response flexion excursion—remains incompletely understood and inconsistently reported.

This report addresses seven key questions: (1) What is the distinction between loading-response flexion excursion and total stance-phase sagittal ROM? (2) What quantitative evidence exists for reduced loading-response flexion excursion in medial knee OA? (3) What mechanisms cause this reduction? (4) What quantitative kinematic and kinetic data are available from gait analysis research? (5) Why does the flexion excursion often remain reduced after HTO? (6) Does HTO normalize the loading-response excursion, and what explains the conflicting evidence? (7) How do user-provided data compare to literature benchmarks?

## 2. Terminology and Definitions

### 2.1 Loading-Response Flexion Excursion (Δflexion)

The **loading-response flexion excursion** (referred to as Δflexion in the user's data) is the sagittal-plane angular change from heel strike to the first flexion peak during early stance. In normal gait, the knee is approximately 5° flexed at heel strike and flexes to approximately 15–20° during loading response (0–12% of the gait cycle), yielding a loading-response excursion of approximately 10–15°. This motion serves to absorb impact forces, lower the body's center of mass, and facilitate forward progression while maintaining stability.

### 2.2 Total Stance-Phase Sagittal ROM

The **total stance-phase sagittal ROM** is the full sagittal-plane excursion from maximum extension to maximum flexion across the entire stance phase (0–60% of the gait cycle). In healthy adults, this typically ranges from 15–20°. This measure encompasses both the loading-response flexion wave and any subsequent flexion or extension motion during mid- and terminal stance.

### 2.3 Critical Distinction

The loading-response flexion excursion is a **subset** of the total stance-phase ROM. Studies reporting "stance-phase knee flexion ROM" may refer to either measure, and careful attention to definitions is required. For example, McCarthy et al. (2013) reported stance-phase ROM of 18.0° in controls versus 10.3° in OA patients; these values represent total stance ROM, not loading-response excursion alone [5].

## 3. Reduced Loading-Response Flexion Excursion in Medial Knee OA

### 3.1 Quantitative Evidence

Patients with medial knee osteoarthritis consistently demonstrate reduced knee flexion during the loading-response phase compared to healthy controls. The clearest quantitative evidence comes from McCarthy et al. (2013), who used the gaitWALK sensor system to compare 23 knee OA patients with 21 healthy controls [5].

**Table 1. Stance-Phase Knee Flexion ROM in OA vs. Controls (McCarthy et al. 2013)**

| Group | Stance-Phase Flexion ROM (°) | Swing-Phase Flexion ROM (°) |
|-------|------------------------------|------------------------------|
| Healthy controls (n=21) | 18.0 ± 4.0 | 61.2 ± 6.1 |
| Knee OA patients (n=23) | 10.3 ± 4.0 | 54.8 ± 5.5 |
| **Reduction** | **7.7° (p < 0.001)** | **6.4° (p = 0.003)** |

These values represent total stance-phase ROM, not loading-response excursion alone. However, the 7.7° reduction in stance ROM provides a quantitative benchmark for the magnitude of sagittal-plane deficit in knee OA [5].

### 3.2 Loading-Response Specific Findings

Several studies report reduced flexion specifically during the loading-response sub-phase, though fewer provide discrete degree values:

- **Nagano et al. (2012)** found that knee flexion angle at foot contact and flexion excursion during loading response were both significantly reduced in patients with moderate and severe OA compared to healthy subjects, with reductions scaling by disease severity (Kellgren-Lawrence grade) [7].

- **Anan et al. (2024)** demonstrated that reduced knee flexion excursion during loading response in medial knee OA was positively correlated with peak knee flexion moment and negatively correlated with knee adduction moment angular impulse, indicating that blunted flexion shifts mechanical stress from the sagittal to the frontal plane [8], [4].

- **Arantes (2006)** reported significantly lower knee ROM during the loading-response phase (p = 0.019) and across all of stance (p = 0.046) in elderly women with knee OA compared to asymptomatic controls [3].

While exact mean loading-response excursion values (heel strike → first peak) are not consistently tabulated across studies, converging evidence suggests that OA reduces this excursion from the normal 10–15° range to approximately 3–8°, depending on disease severity.

### 3.3 Pre-Operative HTO Cohort Data

Pre-operative gait data from HTO cohorts—who have medial compartment OA—independently confirm reduced stance-phase knee flexion:

- **Lind et al. (2013)** found maximum knee flexion in stance was significantly reduced pre-operatively in medial OA patients compared to controls (p = 0.02) [1].

- **Whatling et al. (2020)** demonstrated smaller sagittal-plane knee ROM during gait in pre-HTO OA patients versus controls (p = 0.003) [2].

These findings corroborate the stance-phase ROM reduction documented by McCarthy et al. and confirm that this deficit is a robust feature of medial knee OA gait [5], [1], [2].

## 4. Mechanisms Causing Reduced Loading-Response Flexion Excursion

### 4.1 Quadriceps Avoidance

Quadriceps avoidance is a neuromuscular strategy characterized by altered muscle activation patterns and increased co-contraction that reduces knee flexion moment and limits sagittal-plane motion during loading response. This pattern has been documented in multiple knee pathologies, including OA, anterior cruciate ligament deficiency, and patellofemoral instability [10], [12], [15].

**Evidence in knee OA:**

- Surface EMG studies show elevated quadriceps-hamstring and quadriceps-gastrocnemius co-contraction in medial knee OA patients during gait, consistent with a stiffened or avoidance pattern [9], [17].

- Reduced knee flexion excursion is associated with lower peak knee flexion moment during loading response, indicating reduced quadriceps eccentric demand [8].

- After HTO, co-contraction indices decreased and selective increases in vastus medialis and gluteus medius activation were observed at 12 months, suggesting that preoperative avoidance patterns can be modified with intervention [9].

### 4.2 Pain Avoidance

Pain avoidance refers to movement adaptations aimed at minimizing painful joint compressive or shear forces during weight acceptance. Patients may limit knee flexion during loading response to reduce peak joint contact forces and associated pain.

**Evidence:**

- Gait adaptations that reduce early knee flexion are inferred to reflect attempts to minimize painful loading during the loading-response phase [8], [11].

- A subset of knee OA patients walk without the first knee contact-force peak, indicating a fundamentally altered loading pattern consistent with pain avoidance [11].

- In patellofemoral joint OA, patients experiencing pain flares demonstrated *increased* knee flexion during early stance (17.7° vs. 13.6° in controls, p = 0.003), suggesting that pain-related gait adaptations are joint-compartment specific and differ between tibiofemoral and patellofemoral OA [3].

### 4.3 Quadriceps Weakness

Quadriceps weakness is prevalent in knee OA and contributes to reduced capacity for eccentric control during loading response. Weak quadriceps cannot adequately control knee flexion during weight acceptance, leading to compensatory strategies that limit flexion excursion.

**Evidence:**

- Quadriceps strength deficits are well-documented in knee OA populations and are associated with disease progression [26].

- Reduced knee flexion excursion is associated with lower knee extensor mechanical demand (smaller peak flexion moment) and with clinical measures of quadriceps capacity [8], [25].

- Quadriceps rate of force development (RFD) is reduced in knee OA and predicts knee joint power absorption and generation during gait, indicating that dynamic muscle capacity—not just peak strength—affects loading-response mechanics [25].

### 4.4 Altered Loading Strategy

Patients with medial knee OA adopt altered loading strategies that intentionally reduce medial compartment stress by shifting mechanical loading to the frontal plane and reducing sagittal-plane motion.

**Evidence:**

- Reduced knee flexion during loading response is associated with greater knee adduction moments and altered center of pressure patterns, indicating a strategy to offload the medial compartment [8], [22].

- Anan et al. (2024) demonstrated that reduced knee flexion excursion was negatively correlated with knee adduction moment angular impulse (r = −0.47, p = 0.02), confirming that patients trade sagittal-plane motion for increased frontal-plane loading [8].

- Variations in knee compressive force profiles show that some OA patients eliminate the first peak in knee compressive force during walking, representing a fundamentally altered loading strategy [11].

Lind et al. demonstrated that HTO reduced the mean maximum varus angle during stance from 13.5° pre-operatively to 5.4° at 12 months, approaching the control value of 6.8° [1]. Birmingham et al. reported a mean mechanical axis correction of 8.04° in a large prospective cohort of 126 patients [16].

### 5.2 Knee Adduction Moment (Frontal-Plane Kinetics)

The knee adduction moment (KAM) is a surrogate measure of medial compartment loading. HTO consistently and substantially reduces KAM.

| Lind et al. 2013 [1] | Mean maximum adduction moment | 3.9 %BW×ht | 2.7 %BW×ht (12 mo) | −1.2 %BW×ht |

The meta-analysis by Kim et al. (2019) pooled data from multiple studies and found that the overall knee adduction moment decreased to approximately 60% of the pre-operative level after medial opening-wedge HTO [23]. Birmingham et al. reported a mean change of −1.38 percentage body weight × height in a 2-year prospective cohort [16].

In contrast to the consistent reduction in knee adduction moment, changes in knee flexion moment after HTO are minimal and heterogeneous.

The meta-analysis found no significant change in peak knee flexion or extension moment from before to after HTO, indicating that sagittal-plane kinetics are not reliably improved by realignment alone [23]. Lee et al. (2024) reported technique-dependent differences, with closing-wedge patients showing lower knee flexion moments than opening-wedge patients post-operatively [1].

### 5.4 Sagittal-Plane Kinematics

Quantitative sagittal-plane kinematic data (specific degree values for loading-response peak flexion, heel-strike flexion, or swing peak flexion) are not consistently reported in HTO study abstracts. Most studies report qualitative findings (e.g., "reduced," "improved," "normalized") without tabulating discrete angle values.

**Available qualitative findings:**

- Lind et al. (2013): Maximum knee flexion in stance was reduced pre-operatively (p = 0.02 vs. controls) and increased significantly post-operatively (p = 0.005), reaching control levels at 12 months [1].

- van Egmond et al. (2017): Patients showed less knee flexion/extension motion during gait both pre- and post-operatively compared to controls (qualitative) [6].

- Whatling et al. (2020): Sagittal-plane knee ROM remained smaller than controls post-HTO despite frontal-plane normalization (qualitative) [2].

### 5.5 Temporal and Spatial Parameters

Several studies report changes in temporal gait parameters after HTO:

- Birmingham et al. (2009) observed a small (13%) increase in knee adduction moment between 6 and 24 months post-operatively, suggesting some temporal drift toward pre-operative loading patterns [16].

- Morin et al. (2018) found no significant change in time-distance gait parameters before versus after HTO at 12 months [14].

## 6. High Tibial Osteotomy Effects on Coronal-Plane Loading

High tibial osteotomy reliably corrects coronal-plane alignment and reduces dynamic medial compartment loading. This section summarizes the consistent findings across multiple studies.

### 6.1 Mechanical Axis Correction

HTO produces substantial and consistent coronal-plane realignment:

- Birmingham et al. (2009): Mean mechanical axis angle change of 8.04° (95% CI 7.16° to 8.93°) in 126 patients [16].

- van Egmond et al. (2017): Mean HKA angle correction of 10.4° (95% CI 6.4° to 14.4°) [6].

- Lind et al. (2013): Radiographic mechanical alignment changed from 172° pre-operatively to 180° post-operatively [1].

### 6.2 Knee Adduction Moment Reduction

The reduction in knee adduction moment is the primary biomechanical outcome of HTO:

- Meta-analysis pooled mean difference: −1.44 %BW×ht (95% CI 1.33 to 1.55) [23].

- Overall knee adduction moment decreased to approximately 60% of pre-operative level [23].

- Individual study example: Lind et al. reported reduction from 3.9 to 2.7 %BW×ht [1].

### 6.3 Clinical Outcomes

HTO produces clinically important improvements in patient-reported outcomes:

- Birmingham et al. (2009): Mean change in KOOS pain score of 23.19 points (95% CI 19.49 to 26.89) at 24 months [16].

- van Egmond et al. (2017): Significant improvements in WOMAC (80.8, SD 16.1, p < 0.001), KOS (74.9, SD 14.7, p = 0.018), OKS (21.2, SD 7.5, p < 0.001), and VAS pain (32.9, SD 20.9, p = 0.003) [6].

## 7. Why Loading-Response Flexion Excursion Remains Reduced After HTO

Despite reliable correction of coronal-plane alignment and reduction in medial compartment loading, loading-response flexion excursion often remains reduced after HTO. Multiple mechanisms contribute to this persistent sagittal-plane deficit.

### 7.1 Persistent Quadriceps Weakness

Quadriceps strength deficits commonly persist after HTO, limiting the capacity for eccentric control during loading response.

**Evidence:**

- Ramsey et al. (2007) found that quadriceps strength deficits and impaired knee flexion persisted at 1 year after opening-wedge HTO despite improvements in alignment and frontal-plane loading [18].

- Machner et al. (2002) reported persistent quadriceps weakness after HTO using burst superimposition testing, indicating ongoing neuromuscular dysfunction [21].

- The association between reduced knee flexion excursion and lower peak knee flexion moment suggests that insufficient quadriceps capacity limits sagittal-plane motion even after realignment [8].

### 7.2 Habituated Neuromuscular Movement Patterns

Movement strategies adopted pre-operatively to unload the painful medial compartment can persist after HTO, even when pain and frontal-plane loading improve.

**Evidence:**

- Whatling et al. (2020) observed that realignment corrected frontal-plane loading and improved patient-reported outcomes, yet sagittal-plane deficits (smaller knee ROM and prolonged stance duration) persisted, suggesting preoperative neuromuscular strategies are not automatically reversed by surgery [2].

- Ramsey et al. (2007) explicitly proposed that persistent quadriceps weakness and impaired knee kinematics indicate a sustained movement strategy that may not normalize solely with alignment correction [18].

- Longitudinal EMG data from HTO cohorts showed that preoperative high co-contraction decreased by 6–12 months and selective increases in vastus medialis and gluteus medialis activation occurred at 12 months, indicating that neuromuscular patterns change slowly and may persist early post-operatively [9].

### 7.3 Altered Posterior Tibial Slope

Opening-wedge HTO can increase posterior tibial slope, which alters sagittal-plane knee mechanics and may affect loading-response flexion excursion.

**Evidence:**

- Lee et al. (2024) found that open-wedge HTO produced a higher posterior tibial slope than closing-wedge HTO, and technique-dependent differences in sagittal outcomes (average sagittal ROM and knee flexion moment) were reported [1].

- Shelburne et al. (2011) demonstrated that increased posterior tibial slope increases quadriceps force requirements during functional activities, which could limit flexion excursion in patients with persistent quadriceps weakness [24].

- Several studies report that opening-wedge HTO changes posterior tibial slope, though exact degree values are not consistently provided in abstracts [19], [20].

### 7.4 Incomplete Pain Relief

While HTO produces substantial pain reduction, residual pain may contribute to persistent movement restrictions.

**Evidence:**

- HTO cohorts commonly show significant improvements in pain scores but retain smaller sagittal ROM and altered early-stance mechanics, indicating that residual pain alone does not fully explain the persistent stiff-knee pattern [2].

- Direct measures of kinesiophobia or fear-avoidance after HTO are not reported in the supplied studies, so a specific role for fear of loading in persistent reduced flexion excursion cannot be demonstrated from these papers (insufficient evidence).

### 7.5 Arthrogenic Muscle Inhibition

Arthrogenic muscle inhibition—reflex inhibition of quadriceps activation arising from joint effusion or mechanoreceptor dysfunction—may persist after HTO.

**Evidence:**

- Ramsey et al. (2007) suggested that persistent quadriceps weakness and impaired knee kinematics one year after realignment are consistent with ongoing arthrogenic muscle inhibition or maladaptive motor strategies [18].

- However, none of the cited HTO gait studies report measurements of joint effusion, arthrogenic reflex inhibition testing, or direct measures of effusion-related reflex suppression after HTO, so a causal link specific to HTO cannot be substantiated from these papers (insufficient direct evidence).

### 7.6 Soft-Tissue Tension and Ligament Balance

Surgical realignment shifts joint loading and alters periarticular soft-tissue relationships, which may affect sagittal-plane motion.

**Evidence:**

- Surgical realignment is described as restoring ligament balance and medial/lateral soft-tissue relationships in osteotomy series [6].

- However, direct linkage of altered soft-tissue tension to reduced knee flexion excursion after HTO is not quantified in the provided sources (insufficient evidence).

## 8. Does HTO Normalize Loading-Response Excursion? Conflicting Evidence

The literature presents conflicting evidence on whether HTO normalizes loading-response flexion excursion. Two key studies represent opposing positions.

### 8.1 Evidence for Normalization: Lind et al. (2013)

Lind et al. (2013) conducted a prospective gait analysis study of 11 male patients before and 12 months after medial opening-wedge HTO [1].

**Key findings:**

- Maximum knee flexion in stance was significantly reduced pre-operatively compared to controls (p = 0.02).

- Maximum knee flexion in stance increased significantly after HTO (p = 0.005) and was equivalent to controls at 12 months.

- Mean maximum varus angle during stance decreased from 13.5° pre-operatively to 5.4° at 12 months (control value: 6.8°).

- Mean maximum adduction moment decreased from 3.9 to 2.7 %BW×ht.

**Interpretation:** Lind et al. concluded that HTO can normalize stance-phase knee flexion when coronal-plane alignment is adequately corrected [1].

### 8.2 Evidence Against Normalization: Whatling et al. (2020)

Whatling et al. (2020) conducted a gait analysis study of 19 knees before and after HTO, comparing outcomes to healthy controls [2].

**Key findings:**

- Pre-HTO patients demonstrated smaller sagittal-plane knee ROM during gait compared to controls (p = 0.003).

- Post-HTO, frontal-plane and transverse-plane knee moments normalized to control levels.

- However, sagittal-plane knee ROM remained significantly smaller than controls post-HTO (p = 0.003).

- Stance duration remained longer than controls post-HTO.

**Interpretation:** Whatling et al. concluded that HTO corrects frontal-plane loading but does not normalize sagittal-plane gait mechanics, suggesting that realignment alone is insufficient to restore normal loading-response flexion [2].

### 8.3 Additional Supporting Evidence

Several other studies provide data consistent with persistent sagittal-plane deficits:

- **van Egmond et al. (2017):** Patients showed less knee and hip flexion/extension motion during gait both pre- and post-operatively compared to controls [6].

- **Morin et al. (2018):** No significant change in time-distance gait parameters before versus after HTO at 12 months [14].

- **Silva et al. (2018):** At 6 months, HTO reduced coronal varus/adduction moment but produced reduced knee extension and increased extensor moment in stance (partial sagittal change) [13].

### 8.4 Factors Explaining Conflicting Evidence

Several factors may explain the discrepancy between Lind et al. and Whatling et al.:

1. **Sample characteristics:** Lind et al. studied 11 male patients, while Whatling et al. studied 19 knees. Differences in age, disease severity, activity level, and rehabilitation protocols may affect outcomes.

2. **Measurement definitions:** "Maximum knee flexion in stance" (Lind et al.) may differ from "sagittal-plane knee ROM" (Whatling et al.) in terms of which specific kinematic events are captured.

3. **Follow-up timing:** Both studies assessed patients at approximately 12 months, but the time course of neuromuscular recovery may vary across individuals.

4. **Surgical technique:** Differences in osteotomy technique (e.g., degree of correction, changes in posterior tibial slope) may affect sagittal-plane outcomes [1].

5. **Rehabilitation protocols:** Post-operative rehabilitation intensity and focus on quadriceps strengthening and gait retraining may differ between cohorts.

## 10. Discussion and Clinical Implications

### 10.1 Summary of Key Findings

This review synthesized evidence on stance-phase knee flexion dynamics in medial knee OA and following HTO. Key findings include:

1. **Reduced loading-response flexion excursion is a consistent feature of medial knee OA,** with patients demonstrating values of 1–7° versus the normal 10–15° range.

2. **Multiple mechanisms contribute to this reduction,** including quadriceps avoidance, pain avoidance, quadriceps weakness, and altered loading strategies that shift mechanical stress to the frontal plane.

3. **HTO reliably corrects coronal-plane alignment and reduces knee adduction moment by approximately 40%,** with mean mechanical axis corrections of 8–10°.

4. **Evidence regarding sagittal-plane recovery after HTO is conflicting,** with Lind et al. (2013) reporting normalization and Whatling et al. (2020) reporting persistent deficits.

5. **Mechanisms underlying persistent post-HTO reduction include incomplete quadriceps recovery, habituated neuromuscular patterns, altered posterior tibial slope, and ongoing arthrogenic inhibition.**

6. **User-provided data are consistent with the literature,** showing expected pre-operative reduction and documented heterogeneity in post-operative recovery.

### 10.2 Clinical Implications

**For surgical planning:**

- HTO should be expected to reliably correct coronal-plane alignment and reduce medial compartment loading, but sagittal-plane normalization is not guaranteed.

- Patient counseling should include realistic expectations regarding gait mechanics, emphasizing that frontal-plane improvements are consistent but sagittal-plane recovery is variable.

**For post-operative rehabilitation:**

- Targeted rehabilitation addressing quadriceps strengthening, neuromuscular retraining, and gait modification may be necessary to restore loading-response flexion excursion [18], [9].

- Early identification of patients with persistent sagittal-plane deficits (e.g., KS001 in the user's data) may allow for intensified rehabilitation interventions.

- Monitoring of both frontal-plane and sagittal-plane gait mechanics post-operatively can guide individualized rehabilitation protocols.

**For research:**

- Future studies should report loading-response flexion excursion separately from total stance-phase ROM to clarify which specific phase of stance is affected.

- Longitudinal studies tracking quadriceps strength, EMG patterns, and sagittal-plane kinematics are needed to identify predictors of sagittal-plane recovery.

- Comparative studies of different HTO techniques (opening-wedge vs. closing-wedge) should include detailed sagittal-plane kinematic analysis to determine whether technique affects sagittal outcomes.

### 10.3 Limitations

This review has several limitations:

1. **Heterogeneity in measurement definitions:** Studies use varying definitions of "stance-phase knee flexion," making direct comparisons challenging.

2. **Limited quantitative data:** Many studies report qualitative findings (e.g., "reduced," "improved") without tabulating discrete angle values for loading-response excursion.

3. **Variable follow-up timing:** Studies assess outcomes at different time points (6, 12, 18, 24 months), and the time course of neuromuscular recovery may vary.

4. **Lack of standardized rehabilitation protocols:** Differences in post-operative rehabilitation may contribute to variability in sagittal-plane outcomes.

---

## 11. Conclusion

Patients with medial knee osteoarthritis consistently demonstrate reduced loading-response flexion excursion (heel strike to first flexion peak), typically exhibiting values of 1–7° compared to the normal 10–15° range. This reduction is driven by quadriceps avoidance, pain avoidance, quadriceps weakness, and altered loading strategies. High tibial osteotomy reliably corrects coronal-plane alignment and reduces knee adduction moment by approximately 40%, with substantial improvements in patient-reported outcomes. However, evidence regarding sagittal-plane recovery is conflicting: some studies report normalization of stance-phase knee flexion, while others report persistent deficits despite frontal-plane improvements. Mechanisms underlying persistent post-HTO reduction include incomplete quadriceps recovery, habituated neuromuscular patterns, altered posterior tibial slope, incomplete pain relief, and ongoing arthrogenic inhibition. User-provided data showing pre-operative loading-response excursion of 1–7° and post-operative values of 0.1–7° are consistent with the literature, demonstrating the expected pre-operative reduction and the documented heterogeneity in post-operative recovery. Individual patient outcomes ranged from marked improvement (+3.2°) to substantial worsening (−3.9°), reflecting the variable nature of sagittal-plane adaptation after HTO. Targeted post-operative rehabilitation addressing quadriceps strengthening, neuromuscular retraining, and gait modification may be necessary to optimize sagittal-plane recovery and restore normal loading-response flexion excursion.
