## Literature Benchmarks

### 1. Total Stance-Phase Knee Flexion ROM (McCarthy et al. 2013)

| Group            | Stance-phase flexion ROM (°) |
|------------------|------------------------------|
| Healthy controls | 18.0 ± 4.0                   |
| Knee OA patients | 10.3 ± 4.0                   |
| **Deficit**      | **~7.7° (p < 0.001)**        |

**Important distinction:** These 10.3° and 18.0° values represent the *total* sagittal-plane excursion across the entire stance phase (from maximum extension to maximum flexion), not the loading-response sub-phase alone. Your Δflexion values represent specifically the loading-response flexion excursion (heel strike → first flexion peak), which is a *subset* of total stance ROM.

### 2. Normal Loading-Response Flexion Excursion

In healthy gait, the knee is typically ~5° flexed at heel strike and flexes to ~15–20° during loading response, yielding a **loading-response excursion of approximately 10–15°** (Perry & Burnfield, *Gait Analysis*, standard reference).

### 3. OA Loading-Response Excursion (Literature Estimates)

- **Nagano et al. (2012):** Flexion angle at foot contact and flexion excursion during loading response were both significantly reduced in moderate-to-severe OA vs. healthy subjects, with reductions scaling by disease severity (KL grade). Exact degree values for the loading-response excursion sub-phase were not tabulated.
- **Anan et al. (2024):** Confirmed reduced knee flexion excursion during loading response in medial knee OA, with this excursion positively correlated with knee flexion moment and negatively correlated with KAM angular impulse — linking blunted flexion to altered frontal-plane stress.
- **Arantes (2006):** Reported significantly lower knee ROM during loading response (p = 0.019) in elderly women with knee OA vs. asymptomatic controls.

While no paper in this corpus reports exact mean loading-response excursion in OA in a clean OA-vs-control table, the converging evidence suggests **OA reduces loading-response excursion from the normal ~10–15° range down to roughly 3–8°**, depending on disease severity.

### 4. HTO-Specific Pre/Post Gait Evidence

- **Lind et al. (2013):** Maximum stance-phase knee flexion was significantly reduced pre-HTO (p = 0.02 vs. controls) and improved post-HTO (p = 0.005). Suggests partial normalization.
- **Whatling et al. (2020):** Sagittal-plane knee ROM during gait was significantly smaller pre-HTO (p = 0.003 vs. controls) and **this deficit persisted post-HTO**, suggesting realignment alone does not fully correct the sagittal pattern.

## Comparison: Are Your Results Reasonable?

### Pre-operative values (mean ~4.3°, range 1.0–6.8°)

**Verdict: Reasonable — on the low-to-moderate end of the expected OA spectrum.**

- Your pre-op mean of ~4.3° is substantially below the normal loading-response excursion (~10–15°), which is consistent with the well-documented blunted flexion pattern in medial knee OA.
- It is also below the total stance ROM reported for OA patients (10.3 ± 4.0°, McCarthy et al.), but this is expected because your measure captures only the loading-response sub-phase, not the entire stance ROM.
- The range across subjects (1.0° to 6.8°) reflects the inter-subject variability commonly reported in OA gait studies. KS003 (1.0°) has a nearly abolished loading-response flexion wave — consistent with an extreme quadriceps avoidance / stiff-knee pattern — while KS008 (6.8°) is closer to the lower bound of normal.
- HTO candidates typically present with moderate-to-severe medial OA (KL 3–4), so values at the lower end of the OA range are expected.

### Post-operative values (mean ~4.2°, range 0.1–7.0°)

**Verdict: Reasonable — consistent with the literature's mixed findings on HTO sagittal-plane recovery.**

- The group mean essentially did not change (4.3° → 4.2°), which aligns with **Whatling et al.'s (2020)** finding that sagittal-plane knee ROM deficits persist after HTO.
- However, the individual-level heterogeneity is notable and also supported by the literature:
  - **KS003** improved markedly (1.0° → 4.2°, +3.2°) — consistent with Lind et al.'s finding that some patients do normalize.
  - **KS006** improved modestly (5.5° → 6.8°, +1.3°).
  - **KS008** was essentially unchanged (6.8° → 7.0°, +0.2°).
  - **KS001** worsened substantially (4.0° → 0.1°, −3.9°) — this is an outlier worth investigating. The extremely short time-to-peak post-op (30 ms) and near-zero excursion suggest the loading-response flexion wave may be almost entirely abolished, possibly indicating post-surgical quadriceps inhibition, protective guarding, or measurement artifact.
  - **KS002** decreased slightly (4.1° → 3.0°, −1.0°).

### Timing data (Time HS→peak)

- Your values range from 73–130 ms pre-op and 30–133 ms post-op. Normal time to the first flexion peak is approximately 50–100 ms after heel strike.
- Most of your values fall within or near this window, which is reasonable.
- KS001 post-op at 30 ms is unusually early and paired with the near-zero excursion — this combination suggests the "peak" may essentially be noise rather than a true flexion wave.
- KS008 at 130/133 ms is slightly delayed, possibly reflecting a slower gait speed.

---

## Key Takeaways

1. **Your pre-op values are consistent with the OA gait literature.** A loading-response excursion of ~1–7° in HTO candidates (moderate-to-severe medial OA) aligns with the well-documented pattern of blunted stance-phase knee flexion caused by quadriceps avoidance, pain avoidance, and altered loading strategies.

2. **Your post-op group-level finding of no net improvement is consistent with Whatling et al. (2020),** who reported that sagittal-plane deficits persist after HTO despite frontal-plane moment improvements. This is one of the two major literature positions (the other, represented by Lind et al. 2013, reports some improvement).

3. **The inter-subject variability in your post-op data is a realistic finding,** not a flaw. Both improvement (KS003, KS006) and worsening/no change (KS001, KS002, KS008) patterns are seen in the literature. HTO primarily corrects frontal-plane alignment; sagittal-plane adaptation depends on additional factors (quadriceps recovery, rehabilitation, pain relief, neuromuscular reprogramming).

4. **KS001 post-op warrants scrutiny.** A Δflexion of 0.1° with a 30 ms time-to-peak is at the extreme of the range and may reflect either a genuinely abolished flexion wave (complete quadriceps avoidance) or a data processing artifact. This should be cross-checked against the raw kinematic waveform.
