# Knee Biomechanics During Gait in Patients Undergoing High Tibial Osteotomy (HTO) Using Bifluoscopy (Biplanar Fluoroscopy): A Comprehensive Literature Review

---

## Executive Summary

High tibial osteotomy (HTO) is an established joint-preserving surgical procedure for younger, active patients with medial compartment knee osteoarthritis (OA) and varus malalignment. By realigning the mechanical axis of the lower limb, HTO reduces medial compartment loading and delays the need for total knee arthroplasty. Biomechanical assessment during gait is essential to quantify the functional success of this realignment, with key parameters including the knee adduction moment (KAM), varus angle, walking speed, knee flexion, and tibial rotation.

Conventional motion capture systems, while widely used for gait analysis, suffer from soft-tissue artefact that introduces errors of several millimetres in joint kinematic measurements. Biplanar fluoroscopy (bifluoscopy) overcomes this limitation by simultaneously capturing two X-ray images and matching three-dimensional bone models to them, yielding six degrees-of-freedom (6-DOF) kinematics with sub-millimetre translational accuracy and sub-degree rotational precision. This technology enables precise measurement of tibiofemoral contact patterns, condylar motion, tibial translation and rotation, and the screw-home mechanism during dynamic activities.

This review synthesises the current literature on gait biomechanics in HTO patients, biplanar fluoroscopy methodology, precision kinematics analysis, clinical outcomes, and emerging research trends. The findings contextualise a research programme involving 9 HTO patients with pre- and post-operative bifluoscopy recordings during level and slope walking, highlighting how such data can advance the field toward personalised surgical planning and improved patient outcomes.

## 1. Introduction

Medial compartment knee osteoarthritis(무릎 골관절염, 엑스레이에서 내측 관절 간격이 좁아지며 O자 변형) is a progressive degenerative condition characterised by cartilage loss, subchondral bone remodelling, and increasing pain and disability. In patients with concomitant varus malalignment(비정렬), the mechanical axis of the lower limb passes medial to the knee centre, concentrating load in the medial compartment and accelerating joint degeneration(관절 퇴행). For younger, active individuals who wish to preserve their native joint, high tibial osteotomy (HTO) offers a joint-preserving alternative to arthroplasty by correcting coronal alignment and redistributing mechanical load [13].

The fundamental biomechanical principle underlying HTO is the ==transfer of load from the degenerated medial compartment to the relatively preserved lateral compartment==. This is achieved by creating an osteotomy in the proximal tibia and either opening the medial cortex (medial opening-wedge HTO) or closing a lateral wedge, thereby shifting the weight-bearing line (WBL) laterally. The classical correction target positions the WBL at approximately 62.5% of the tibial plateau width(경골의 상단면), passing through the lateral compartment while preserving near-normal knee kinematics [8].

Quantifying the functional success of HTO requires biomechanical assessment during dynamic activities, particularly gait. Three-dimensional gait analysis provides objective measurement of joint angles, moments, and temporal-spatial parameters that cannot be captured by static radiographic assessment alone. Key biomechanical parameters of interest include the ==external knee adduction moment (KAM, the primary surrogate for medial compartment loading)==, ==coronal plane varus angle==, ==sagittal plane knee flexion==, ==walking speed==, and ==axial tibial rotation== [1], [13].

While conventional optical motion capture has been the standard tool for clinical gait analysis, it is limited by soft-tissue artefact—the relative motion between skin-mounted markers and underlying bone—which introduces translational errors of several millimetres. Biplanar fluoroscopy (bifluoscopy) overcomes this fundamental limitation by directly imaging skeletal structures, enabling sub-millimetre precision in measuring 6-DOF knee kinematics during functional activities [16]. The integration of bifluoscopy with HTO assessment represents a frontier in precision orthopaedics, enabling clinicians and researchers to understand how surgical realignment affects the intricate mechanics of the tibiofemoral and patellofemoral joints at a level previously unattainable.

## 2. Gait Analysis in HTO Patients

### 2.1 Pre-operative Gait Characteristics

Patients with medial compartment OA and varus malalignment demonstrate characteristic gait abnormalities prior to HTO. Reduced walking speed, ==decreased knee flexion during stance==, ==elevated varus angles==, and increased medial loading (reflected by elevated KAM) are hallmark features. In a controlled study of 11 male patients, Lind et al. documented that pre-operative maximum knee flexion in stance was significantly reduced compared to age-matched controls (p = 0.02), mean maximum varus angle reached 13.5° during stance (versus 6.8° in controls), and peak KAM was 3.9 %BW/ht (versus 3.6 in controls) [1]. Skvortsov et al. similarly reported significant kinematic deviations in the coronal and sagittal planes in patients with medial knee arthritis prior to corrective valgus osteotomy [6].

### 2.2 Post-operative Kinematic and Kinetic Changes

HTO produces substantial and clinically meaningful improvements across multiple biomechanical parameters:

**Varus angle correction:** The most immediate biomechanical effect of HTO is reduction of the dynamic varus angle during stance. Lind et al. reported a decrease from 13.5° preoperatively to 5.4° postoperatively, with the corrected value closely approximating controls (6.8°). Mean radiological mechanical alignment changed from 172° to 180° [1].

**Knee adduction moment:** The KAM, widely accepted as a surrogate for medial compartment loading, is consistently reduced following HTO. Mean peak KAM decreased from 3.9 to 2.7 %BW/ht in one series [1]. In patients undergoing combined HTO with ACL reconstruction, the first-peak external KAM was significantly reduced at both 2 and 5 years postoperatively [2]. Medium-term follow-up using gait analysis for dynamic correction demonstrated statistically significant KAM reduction maintained at a mean of 6 years [4].

**Walking speed and knee flexion:** Walking speed normalised postoperatively and was not significantly different from controls (p = 0.0001 for pre-to-post improvement). Maximum knee flexion in stance increased significantly (p = 0.005), equalling control values, as did the peak knee flexion moment [1].

**Compartmental contact forces:** Inverse-dynamic modelling demonstrated that medial open-wedge HTO improved balance between medial and lateral compartment contact forces and significantly reduced lateral-medial shear force during stance phase [7]. These findings were further supported by combined gait analysis, materials testing, and dynamic radiography investigations [31].

### 2.3 Temporal Recovery Pattern

The temporal dynamics of biomechanical recovery after HTO follow a characteristic pattern. Prizov et al. investigated gait and knee kinematics before surgery and at 6 and 18 months postoperatively, finding that the ==most substantial functional and biomechanical gains occur within the first six months==. Between 6 and 18 months, moderate further patient-reported improvement was observed, but no significant additional biomechanical change was detectable [3]. This finding has implications for rehabilitation protocols and the timing of post-operative assessments.

### 2.4 Effects on Tibial Rotation and the Contralateral Limb

HTO produces important secondary biomechanical effects beyond simple coronal plane correction:

**Tibial rotation and 3-D kinematics:** MRI-based three-dimensional analysis demonstrated that ==opening-wedge HTO produces increased tibial anterior translation (mean 2.6 mm, p < 0.001)==, decreased patellar proximal translation (−2.2 mm, p < 0.001), and altered patellar spin and tilt [12]. Computer modelling by Kuriyama et al. confirmed that increasing the ==WBL percentage progressively reduces the screw-home movement== and the tibiofemoral rotational angle during flexion, with over-correction to WBL 80% producing non-physiological kinematics [8].

**Muscle activation changes:** Post-operative neuromuscular adaptations include decreased co-contraction indices of vastus medialis–biceps femoris and vastus medialis–gastrocnemius at 6 and 12 months, while gluteus medius and vastus medialis activation intensity and duration increased, indicating improved lower-limb coordination [11].

**Contralateral limb effects:** An important and often overlooked finding is that the non-operated knee may experience increased loading after unilateral HTO. Lind et al. documented that adduction moments in the non-operated knee increased postoperatively from 3.3 to 4.1 %BW/ht (p = 0.02) [1].

## 3. Bifluoscopy/Biplanar Fluoroscopy Methodology

### 3.1 Principles of Dual-Plane Fluoroscopic Imaging

Biplanar fluoroscopic imaging systems (also termed dual fluoroscopic imaging systems, DFIS, or biplanar fluoroscopy, BPF) capture two simultaneous X-ray images from different viewing angles and match three-dimensional bone models to these projections, yielding six degrees-of-freedom (6-DOF) kinematics—three translations (anterior-posterior, medial-lateral, proximal-distal) and three rotations (flexion-extension, internal-external rotation, abduction-adduction) [16].

The fundamental workflow involves: (1) acquisition of patient-specific 3D bone models from computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); (2) simultaneous capture of biplanar fluoroscopic images during dynamic activity; and (3) matching of 3D bone model projections to the captured fluoroscopic images using model-based tracking algorithms. The key advantage over conventional motion capture is that skeletal motion is measured directly from bone contours, eliminating soft-tissue artefact entirely [16], [24].

Early implementations by Li et al. (2009) integrated a DFIS with a treadmill, demonstrating the feasibility of capturing 6-DOF knee kinematics during gait at speeds ranging from 1.5 to 3.0 MPH. The system captured three strides at each speed, revealing repeatable motion patterns with flexion amplitudes increasing with walking speed while motion patterns in other degrees of freedom remained largely unaffected [24].

### 3.2 Validation Accuracy

The measurement accuracy of biplanar fluoroscopy has been rigorously characterised across multiple systems and configurations:

**Dual Fluoroscopic Imaging System (DFIS):** Li et al. (2008) validated the DFIS using both phantom objects and cadaver specimens. Static translational pose recreation was accurate to less than 0.15 ± 0.09 mm. In cadaver knees undergoing dynamic flexion-extension, differences were 0.24 ± 0.16 mm for posterior femoral translation and 0.16 ± 0.61° for internal-external tibial rotation. High repeatability was demonstrated for in vivo step ascent and treadmill gait [16].

**Model-based biplane fluoroscopy (prosthesis tracking):** Barré et al. (2018) characterised errors of a biplane fluoroscopic system tracking knee prostheses during treadmill gait. Static maximum errors were 0.06 mm for position and 0.13° for orientation. Dynamic errors reached 2.42 mm for position (vertical axis) and 0.95° for orientation (medio-lateral axis), with accuracy improving near the system centroid [19].

**High-Speed Stereo-Radiography (HSSR):** Ivester et al. (2015) developed a reconfigurable gantry HSSR system designed for sub-millimetre accuracy while allowing freedom of subject movement and integration into existing motion capture laboratories [18].

**Biplane Dynamic Roentgen Stereogrammetric Analysis (DRSA):** Papaioannou et al. (2009) achieved average dynamic errors of ±0.1 mm using a marker-based tracking method combined with a new DRSA system, making it one order of magnitude more accurate than conventional motion analysis techniques [29].

**Model-based biplane radiography (sequences):** You et al. (2001) reported in vitro accuracy of 0.5 mm translation and 1.4° rotation, with in vivo testing yielding differences of 0.8 mm for translation and 2.5° for rotation [32].

### 3.3 Comparison with Conventional Motion Capture

Conventional optical motion capture systems, while providing full-body kinematic data over unlimited gait cycles, suffer from soft-tissue artefact (STA) that can introduce errors of 10–30 mm at the thigh and shank segments. ==This fundamentally limits the precision of secondary kinematic measures such as internal-external rotation and anterior-posterior translation.== ==Biplanar fluoroscopy eliminates this artefact by imaging bone directly,== providing measurements that serve as the gold standard for validating skin-marker-based techniques [18], [29].

The primary limitations of biplanar fluoroscopy relative to conventional motion capture include: (1) restricted field of view (FOV), which historically confined measurement to a limited portion of the gait cycle; (2) radiation exposure; (3) expense and complexity of equipment; and (4) requirement for patient-specific 3D bone models [24], [25]. However, recent technological advances—particularly tracking fluoroscopes and statistical shape modelling—are systematically addressing these limitations.

## 4. Precision Kinematics Analysis

### 4.1 Tibiofemoral Contact Patterns

Biplanar fluoroscopy enables direct visualisation of ==tibiofemoral contact patterns by determining the closest point between femoral condyle and tibial plateau surface models==. This provides insight into compartmental loading distribution that is directly relevant to HTO outcomes. ==Swain et al. used biplane video X-ray to measure **contact point translations** during gait and stair ascent, demonstrating increased medial-lateral translation range in the medial compartment during these activities [21]==. Kozanek et al. characterised condylar contact patterns during the stance phase of gait, establishing that knee kinematics are fundamentally activity-dependent and that motion patterns from non-weightbearing activities cannot be generalised to gait [23].

In the context of HTO, the ==surgical shift of the weight-bearing line from medial to lateral is expected to alter these contact patterns.== Inverse-dynamic modelling has confirmed improved balance between medial and lateral compartment contact forces after medial opening-wedge HTO [7]. ==The ability to directly measure these contact pattern changes using biplanar fluoroscopy represents a critical advancement in evaluating HTO effectiveness at the articular level.==

### 4.2 Condylar Motion

==Condylar motion during gait encompasses both the medial and lateral femoral condyle translations on the tibial plateau.== Biplanar fluoroscopy studies have revealed that the ==medial condyle acts as a relatively stationary pivot point== while the ==lateral condyle undergoes greater posterior translation during flexion==—the so-called **"medial pivot" pattern** [23].

Okamoto et al. specifically investigated condylar motion after hemicallotasis osteotomy (a variant of opening-wedge HTO) using fluoroscopic image-matching during step-up activity. They found the ==lateral femoral condyle shifted 10 ± 1 mm from flexion to extension preoperatively and 9 ± 1 mm postoperatively==, with no statistically significant differences in anteroposterior femoral condyle movement, suggesting that the medial pivot movement may decrease after HTO due to the shift of the centre of gravity toward the lateral side of the joint line [27].

### 4.3 Tibial Translation and Rotation

==Tibial anterior-posterior translation and internal-external rotation are critical kinematic parameters affected by HTO.== Three-dimensional analysis has demonstrated that opening-wedge HTO produces a mean increase in tibial anterior translation of 2.6 mm (p < 0.001), which has implications for ACL loading and patellofemoral mechanics [12].

Biplanar fluoroscopy studies in healthy knees have established normative reference functions for coupled tibial translation and rotation during gait. Koo et al. characterised these three-dimensional kinematic couplings during treadmill walking, demonstrating that ==tibial anterior translation follows a linear relationship with flexion angle during both swing (y = 0.20x − 3.09) and stance (y = 0.31x − 0.54)==, while ==tibial external rotation follows a quadratic relationship during swing (y = −0.002x² + 0.19x − 0.64) and a linear relationship during stance (y = −0.19x − 1.22).== These coupled motions include the characteristic lateral pivoting motion of the normal knee [22].

Gale et al. established healthy adult normative data using biplane radiography at 100 images/second during treadmill walking in 39 knees, reporting average ranges of motion of 7.0 mm anterior-posterior, 3.2 mm medial-lateral, 2.9 mm proximal-distal for translations, and 67.3° flexion-extension, 11.5° internal-external rotation, and 3.7° abduction-adduction for rotations [20].

### 4.4 Screw-Home Mechanism

The screw-home mechanism—the coupled external tibial rotation that accompanies terminal knee extension—is a fundamental kinematic feature of the normal knee that has implications for joint stability and articular cartilage loading. Computer modelling by Kuriyama et al. demonstrated that this mechanism is sensitive to the degree of HTO correction. ==Increasing the WBL percentage progressively reduced the screw-home movement and the tibiofemoral rotational angle during flexion.== The classical correction target of WBL 62.5% preserved near-normal screw-home kinematics, whereas over-correction to WBL 80% produced excessive lateral loading and abnormal medial condyle–eminence contact, resulting in non-physiological kinematics [8].

This finding has important clinical implications: while greater lateral transfer of load is sometimes advocated for more severe medial OA, excessive correction may compromise rotational kinematics and ==potentially lead to lateral compartment degeneration==. Biplanar fluoroscopy is uniquely suited to detect these subtle rotational changes in vivo, given its sub-degree accuracy for internal-external rotation measurement [16].

## 5. Clinical Outcomes

### 5.1 Patient-Reported Outcome Measures

Clinical improvement following HTO is well-documented across multiple patient-reported outcome measures. The Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) decreased substantially from a preoperative mean of 49.6 (SD 16.4) to 22.5 (SD 14.4) at 12 months after opening-wedge HTO [12]. KSS (Knee Society Score), KOOS (Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score), and VAS (Visual Analogue Scale) scores all showed moderate dynamic improvement, with the most substantial gains occurring within the first six months [3].

Importantly, clinical scores and biomechanical parameters may not always align temporally. Börjesson et al. demonstrated that gait speed serves as a more sensitive detector of functional differences than standard clinical scores, distinguishing HTO from unicompartmental knee arthroplasty outcomes as early as 3 months postoperatively, whereas conventional patient-reported measures could not [15].

### 5.2 Survivorship and Long-Term Results

Long-term survivorship data for HTO informed by gait analysis provide important prognostic information. McClelland et al. reported medium-term results using gait analysis for dynamic correction with an external fixator, demonstrating 89% survivorship at a mean 6-year follow-up with statistically significant KAM reduction. However, long-term survivorship declined to 59% at approximately 15.9 years, highlighting the time-limited nature of HTO as a joint-preserving procedure [4].

### 5.3 Combined HTO Procedures

Combined HTO with anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction represents an important surgical strategy for patients with both varus malalignment and ligamentous instability. Marriott et al. conducted the most comprehensive longitudinal assessment, performing 3-dimensional gait analysis preoperatively and at 2 and 5 years postoperatively in 33 patients. Their results demonstrated sustained reduction in peak KAM and improved transverse-plane moments at 5-year follow-up, supporting the durability of the combined procedure [2].

Personalised osteotomy guide plates represent an emerging innovation in HTO surgical technique. Patients receiving personalised guides achieved superior early gait parameters at 3 months (step frequency 1.89 vs. 1.80 steps/s; step speed 0.99 vs. 0.95 m/s) and better WBL correction compared to traditional techniques [14].

## 6. Current Research Trends

### 6.1 Tracking Dual-Plane Fluoroscopes

The most significant recent technological advance in biplanar fluoroscopy is the development of the tracking dual-plane fluoroscope (tDPF). This system uses optical tracking and model-predictive control so that the X-ray sources follow the knee joint in real time, eliminating the need for pre-recorded movement templates and the traditional restriction to treadmill-based assessments. The tDPF enables continuous 6-DOF measurement across complete gait cycles during level walking, ramp ascent/descent, and stair ascent/descent at self-selected speeds. Tracking performance is excellent, with the knee centre remaining within the FOV for >99% of frames [25].

This technology represents a paradigm shift from stationary systems that could only capture a limited window of the gait cycle, to systems capable of measuring joint kinematics across entire movement cycles during variable-speed, multi-task activities. The clinical impact of such advances is evidenced by recent applications, including biplanar fluoroscopy analysis of residual kinematics after combined ACL and anterolateral ligament reconstruction during walking [30].

### 6.2 Mobile Biplane X-Ray Systems

Mobile biplane X-ray imaging (MBXI) systems permit over-ground walking measurement rather than treadmill walking, which is more representative of natural gait patterns. Guan et al. demonstrated the feasibility of quantifying in vivo knee kinematics and joint centre of rotation during TKA gait using mobile biplane X-ray imaging, establishing a model for HTO application [26]. Over-ground measurement captures natural gait initiation, termination, and speed variability that treadmill protocols cannot replicate.

### 6.3 Statistical Shape Modelling

The requirement for patient-specific CT-based 3D bone models has been a significant barrier to widespread adoption of biplanar fluoroscopy. Statistical shape modelling (SSM) offers an alternative by generating subject-specific bone geometries from standardised fluoroscopic images without additional CT scanning. Li et al. (2014) demonstrated that combining DFIS with ==SSM-derived models yielded kinematic RMS errors within 3.3° for rotation and 2.4 mm for translation compared to CT-based reference models during treadmill walking.== Geometric RMS errors between SSM and CT-based knee models were 1.16 mm for the femur and 1.40 mm for the tibia [17].

This approach substantially reduces cost and radiation exposure while maintaining clinically acceptable accuracy, potentially enabling broader application of precision kinematics measurement in clinical practice.

### 6.4 Machine Learning Prognostication

Machine learning approaches are emerging for preoperative prediction of HTO outcomes. Iwasaki et al. (2026) developed prognostic models using wavelet-derived gait features to predict knee OA outcomes after HTO, potentially enabling preoperative identification of patients most likely to benefit from surgery [9]. This approach leverages the rich temporal and spectral information contained in gait waveforms to train classification and regression models, moving beyond simple peak values toward comprehensive characterisation of movement patterns.

### 6.5 Slope and Stair Walking Analysis

Analysis of knee biomechanics during ==challenging locomotor tasks==—slope walking, stair ascent and descent—provides information about joint function ==under higher loading conditions== that level walking alone cannot reveal. The tDPF system now enables 6-DOF measurement during these activities [25]. Hybrid closed-wedge HTO is also under investigation for its distinct loading effects during gait in medial OA patients, reflecting interest in understanding how different osteotomy techniques perform under various functional demands [10].

Jiang et al. used dual fluoroscopic imaging to investigate patellar kinematics during staircase motion after opening-wedge HTO, finding patellar tendon contraction, shortening of the medial patellotibial ligament, and changes in the quadriceps moment arm that contributed to altered patellofemoral mechanics [28]. Dynamic stereo X-ray has also been applied to investigate whether knee OA with self-reported instability alters translatory tibiofemoral arthrokinematics during the loading response phase of gait, demonstrating the sensitivity of the technique for detecting subtle kinematic differences [21].

## 7. Implications for Future Research

### 7.1 Contribution of 9-Patient Pre/Post-Operative Bifluoscopy Data

A study involving 9 HTO patients with pre- and post-operative bifluoscopy recordings during level and slope walking addresses several critical gaps in the current literature:

**Direct measurement of 6-DOF kinematic changes after HTO:** While most HTO gait studies rely on conventional motion capture (limited by STA), or computational modelling, direct biplanar fluoroscopic measurement of pre- and post-operative knee kinematics in HTO patients is exceedingly rare. Only Okamoto et al. have published fluoroscopic kinematics data in a proximal tibial osteotomy cohort, and that study was limited to a single-plane lateral view during step-up activity in 6 patients [27]. A 9-patient cohort with full biplanar imaging represents a meaningful contribution.

**Paired within-subject design:** The pre- and post-operative design allows each patient to serve as their own control, eliminating inter-individual variability and enabling detection of subtle kinematic changes that might be obscured in between-group comparisons.

**Precision beyond conventional gait analysis:** Sub-millimetre accuracy enables quantification of tibial anterior translation changes (expected ~2.6 mm based on MRI studies [12]), rotational alterations, condylar contact shifts, and screw-home mechanism modifications that conventional motion capture cannot reliably detect.

### 7.2 Level and Slope Walking Protocol

The inclusion of slope walking alongside level walking is particularly significant:

**Increased loading demands:** Slope walking increases knee joint moments and muscle activation requirements, potentially revealing kinematic differences that are not apparent during level walking. This may ==unmask residual joint instability or suboptimal correction ==that functions adequately under less demanding conditions.

**Clinical relevance:** Patients report difficulty with slopes and stairs as primary functional limitations. Measuring kinematics during these activities provides data directly relevant to patient-reported disability.

**Novel data contribution:** While the tDPF system has demonstrated the feasibility of tracking joint kinematics during slope walking in healthy subjects [25], no published study has applied biplanar fluoroscopy to HTO patients during slope walking. This represents a genuine first in the literature and an opportunity to characterise how surgical realignment affects knee mechanics under graduated loading conditions.

**Reference for surgical planning:** Slope walking data can inform alignment targets by revealing whether the classical WBL 62.5% correction optimally distributes load during challenging activities, or whether task-specific correction strategies might be warranted.

### 7.3 Integration with Surgical Planning

The emerging paradigm of integrating instrumental gait analysis with patient-specific tibial plateau modelling for HTO planning has been identified as a promising direction, though no study has yet fully realised this integration [5]. Bifluoscopy data from pre- and post-operative assessments can contribute to this goal by:

1. Providing ground-truth kinematic data for validating computational models of HTO correction
2. Identifying kinematic predictors of clinical success or failure
3. Informing personalised correction targets based on individual joint mechanics
4. Establishing reference kinematics for different correction magnitudes during functional tasks

Combined with machine learning approaches [9] and personalised surgical guides [14], precision kinematics data could enable a fully personalised HTO planning pipeline where the surgical correction is optimised not just for static alignment but for dynamic joint function across multiple activities.

## 8. Conclusion

This review demonstrates that HTO effectively reduces medial compartment loading, normalises dynamic knee function parameters including ==walking speed==, ==knee flexion==, and ==varus angle==, with benefits detectable as early as 6 months and largely stable at 18 months postoperatively. However, important secondary effects—including ==altered tibial rotation==, ==changes in screw-home mechanics==, and ==increased contralateral knee loading==—require precision measurement techniques to fully characterise.

==Biplanar fluoroscopy provides the sub-millimetre, 6-DOF measurement capability necessary to capture these subtle but clinically important kinematic changes.== Validation studies consistently demonstrate translational accuracy within 0.1–0.5 mm and rotational accuracy within 0.2–1.0° for optimally configured systems. Recent advances—tracking dual-plane fluoroscopes, mobile systems, and statistical shape modelling—are systematically addressing historical limitations of restricted field of view, treadmill confinement, and radiation exposure.

The intersection of HTO assessment and biplanar fluoroscopy remains largely unexplored, with only isolated studies applying fluoroscopic techniques to osteotomy patients. A study involving 9 HTO patients with pre- and post-operative bifluoscopy recordings during both level and slope walking is positioned to make a significant contribution by: (1) providing the first biplanar fluoroscopy characterisation of 6-DOF kinematic changes during slope walking after HTO; (2) enabling direct measurement of tibiofemoral contact pattern changes, condylar motion alterations, and screw-home mechanism modifications; and (3) generating precision kinematic data that can inform computational modelling, machine learning prognostication, and personalised surgical planning. Such work bridges the gap between the well-established biomechanical benefits of HTO documented by conventional gait analysis and the emerging era of precision kinematics-guided surgical planning.
